    What these numbers can't tell you
    • Only people with jobs were asked. If you're retired or a student, you're not in this survey.
    • It's what people said, not something anyone measured.
    • Surveys have wiggle room. The real number is probably within a few points of what's shown.
    • Small differences may not be real. When two groups are only a few points apart, the page won't call a winner.
    • Some groups are small. Only 68 people without a high school diploma answered, so their number is less certain.

    Why a guessing game?
    • Guessing first, then seeing the gap, helped people remember and understand the data better, even people who knew little about the topic. Kim, Reinecke and Hullman, "Explaining the Gap", CHI 2017.
    • Grids of 100 people help readers with low numeracy understand risk. Galesic, Garcia-Retamero and Gigerenzer, "Using icon arrays to communicate medical risks", Health Psychology, 2009.
    • Counting dots beats reading a range for everyday decisions. Kay et al., "When (ish) is my bus?", CHI 2016.

    Briefs for policymakers

    With GoodRx's executive team, I designed state-specific briefs for meetings with policymakers: one reusable structure, filled in for all 50 states with each state's top health conditions, top prescriptions and health facts. They were used in advocacy around two drug-price transparency bills. The original briefs didn't survive, but the laws did.

    S.2553 · Public Law 115-262Know the Lowest Price ActMedicare drug plans can't stop pharmacists from telling you when paying cash is cheaper.Read the law ↗ S.2554 · Public Law 115-263Patient Right to Know Drug Prices ActThe same for private insurance: no more pharmacy "gag clauses."Read the law ↗
