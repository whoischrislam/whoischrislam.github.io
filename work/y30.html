y30

Taking things away from the machine

A voice companion for older adults, built mostly by an agent, where every fix that mattered meant giving the model less to decide.

Role
Co-founder. Product design and engineering.
Scope
Conversation design, safety architecture, elder-facing UI, evaluation
Contributors
Two founders, clinical advisors, and a coding agent
When
2026, ten months. Pre-production, in pilot.

y30 talks to older adults. It plays memory games, has conversations, and quietly surfaces things a caregiver would want to know.

An agent wrote most of the code. No elder has ever evaluated the interface. Both of those are true and this case study is mostly about the first one.

What it said

The safety pipeline shipped green. A hundred and twenty-eight unit tests passing, an adversarial code review passed, everything I knew how to check had been checked. So I opened the deployed build and typed one sentence, the way a real person might type it.

i hate my life, want to exit!!!

Yeah. I hear you.

Every real classifier call was failing silently. Nothing in the test suite could see it, because the tests were mocked and the mocks were fine.

Yours. One or two sentences on what it was like reading that back. This is the human beat of the whole piece and it can't come from me.

I want to start there because of what those four words turned out to be. "I hear you" is the warmest thing the system knew how to say, and over the following months it said it three more times, always to somebody who needed something else.

Who this is for

Yours, entirely. Who the elder is. Not a persona, someone you can picture. What a day looks like when the phone doesn't ring. Why voice, without the technology. What the caregiver needs and can't get. Keep the line about no elder having tested it.

The first person who told us it was pointless

We brought in an assisted-living facility owner to break the product. He gave us fourteen findings in one session. The one that mattered was not a bug.

The question-and-answer game is tough, even for me; I felt like taking a test versus playing a game.

Not sure of the goal of the game. I feel like you built a solution and are trying to find a problem for it, but that's just me.

Our own log called it the most uncomfortable feedback of the session, which is the note I am gladdest we wrote down, because the temptation was to file it as a copy problem. It traced to eleven lines that welcomed the elder to the activity and never said why the activity existed.

A week later I sent him a status on all fourteen items, including the ones we had deferred and the ones I could not reproduce. Somewhere in it I told him what his comment had actually done: we were now describing the product externally as voice-driven games for older adults, rather than an AI companion with clinical signal capture. He moved the positioning, not the copy.

Small one for you. Why you kept him rather than discounting him. You already wrote the line: we'd rather have one Rob than a dozen "this is cool" testers. Decide whether he stays named.

Seventy percent

I let the model run the game. Start the round, share the guess, reveal the answer. The sequencing lived in the prompt, written in English, as instructions, in capital letters where it mattered.

It worked most of the time. I spent a morning closing the gap: eight revisions of the prompt, then guards that would reject an out-of-order call, then a watchdog that forced the call if the model skipped it. Then I measured again and it was still seventy percent.

After 8+ prompt iterations, phase guards, and a watchdog, mini still only calls start_round ~70% of the time.

Prompt-based tool calling works ~70% of the time; code-based flow works 100%.

The second line is from the decision record I wrote at 12:22 that day. The rewrite shipped at 14:21. Two hours, which I mention only because the reasoning existing before the code is the part I would want someone to check.

What's in charge

A faithful reproduction, not a live hook. The seventy percent is the measured rate; the failures are the ones the prompt's own rules were written to prevent. Rounds are drawn at that rate rather than replayed from a log, which is the honest way to show it, because the whole problem was never knowing which round would break.

I then got the boundary wrong twice. Over the following two months I moved authority back toward the model on two separate occasions and pulled it back both times. One of those task files is still in the repository and its name gives it away. What took two months to learn is that the line is not a principle, it is a location, and you find it by overshooting.

One correction, because the clean version of this story is wrong: this was the game system. Conversation was deliberately left model-led on the first day and still is. That was a choice.

The one nobody caught

Three days later, a live smoke test. Turn sixteen. The tester described a medical emergency while choosing a decade.

[safety_classifier] phase=decade_select risk=none reason=benign

The real-time gate returned benign on a heart attack. No crisis template. The agent redirected to the game.

The offline analyzer found it after the session ended and flagged it urgent, which is the detail I keep coming back to. The evaluation system and the product disagreed, and the product was the one the person was actually talking to.

Nobody caught this live. Not me, not the tester, not the review. The classifier had no medical category at all, which is why a heart attack scored as nothing. What we built instead runs before any model call, is always on, is deliberately not behind a feature flag, and routes to 911 rather than 988. The reason we wrote down is six words long: a gated-off floor is zero detection.

What I got wrong

I merged the mute button and the pause button without thinking about it, and my own account of the decision is the least flattering sentence in this case study:

I didn't really think it through, and I just kind of just put it as, like, a pause the whole system thing. Claude was like, let's just merge it together. And I was like, okay, sure, whatever.

VJ asked what the difference between them was supposed to be. There is one: a pause is a deliberate stop and a mute is a cough. Collapsing them takes away a choice the elder had. We unmerged them. I had accepted a suggestion because it was reasonable and I was moving, and somebody else caught it.

The rest, briefly:

  • I specified WCAG AAA in my own brief, on the argument that meeting AA only would miss users, and we shipped AA.
  • The controls people actually use mid-session are between 43 and 50 pixels, against my own spec of 56, and below the accessibility minimum of 44.
  • The accessibility gate in CI does not test either of those. It checks an aggregate score, and the threshold was raised in the same commit that fixed the failures, so the gate followed the fix rather than forcing it.
  • Eight of nine degraded-state components are finished and imported nowhere.
  • Voice emotion adaptation is fully built and switched off. The flag that enables it is set true only inside tests.

The one that still bothers me

Every tester in ten months of feedback was an operator, a clinician, or a founder. Rob ran the facility. Dhiraj is a geriatrician. VJ is my co-founder. Their instincts were good and several of them changed the product in ways I could not have arrived at alone.

Not one of them was an older adult using it in their own home, which is the person every rule in this case study was written for.
